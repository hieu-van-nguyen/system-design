Deep Dive — Surge Pricing & Analytics

lesson
⏱ 14 min
📖 Chapter 29, Lesson 4

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Compute a per-cell demand:supply ratio over a rolling window and derive a capped, smoothed surge multiplier
  • Design the streaming pipeline (Kafka → windowed aggregation → multiplier cache) that keeps this fresh at scale
  • Explain why fares are locked at quote time and the risk that creates
  • Justify real-time vs. batch computation for different pieces of the pricing pipeline

Deep Dive — Surge Pricing & Analytics

Lesson 3 already gave surge its trigger: when a cell's shortlist is exhausted (radius expanded to the max and still no accept), that's a live demand-exceeds-supply signal. Surge pricing formalizes that signal into a continuously-updated multiplier per geo-cell, computed from the same location and ride-request data already flowing through the system.

1. Defining the Signal: Demand:Supply per Cell

For each geohash cell (same ~1km cells as the driver location index, Lesson 2):
  demand  = ride requests originating in this cell, last 5 minutes
  supply  = available (not "assigned"/"en_route") drivers currently in this cell

  ratio = demand / max(supply, 1)   // avoid divide-by-zero when supply is 0

Example: cell has 40 requests in 5 min, 10 available drivers → ratio = 4.0

A rolling window (5 minutes) rather than an instantaneous snapshot smooths out noise from individual ride requests arriving in bursts — a single spike of 3 requests in one second shouldn't swing prices, but a sustained imbalance should.

2. From Ratio to Multiplier: Caps and Smoothing

multiplier = clamp(1.0 + log(ratio), 1.0, 3.0)   // caps: never below 1.0x, never above 3.0x
new_multiplier = 0.7 * old_multiplier + 0.3 * multiplier   // exponential smoothing

// log() compresses the impact of extreme ratios (ratio=20 shouldn't be 20x);
// smoothing prevents the multiplier from jumping every time the window recomputes,
// which would make fares visibly flicker between consecutive rider requests seconds apart.
💡 Interview signal: Naming both a cap (business/regulatory reasons — no 20x surge) and smoothing (product reasons — riders shouldn't see the price jump between two requests 10 seconds apart) shows you're thinking beyond "just multiply by the ratio."

3. Streaming Pipeline

Ride requests ──┐
                 ├──► Kafka topic "pricing_events" ──► Stream processor
Driver status ───┘        (demand + supply events)     (5-min tumbling/sliding
                                                          window, keyed by geohash cell)
                                                                 │
                                                                 ▼
                                                    Multiplier cache (Redis)
                                                    key: surge:{geohash} → multiplier
                                                                 │
                                                                 ▼
                                                    Pricing Service reads on
                                                    every fare quote (O(1) lookup)

This mirrors Lesson 2's split between the hot live-location path and the cold analytics path: the windowed aggregation is real-time (seconds of lag, driven by Kafka + a stream processor like Flink/Kafka Streams), while long-term surge-pattern analysis (e.g., predicting tomorrow's rush-hour surge) runs as an offline batch job against the same event stream, feeding a separate historical model rather than the live cache.

4. Price-Lock at Quote Time

The multiplier fluctuates continuously, but a rider's fare must not change after they've committed to a ride:

requestRide() → read surge:{geohash} multiplier NOW → compute quoted_fare
             → store quoted_fare on the ride record (immutable once matched)
             → rider sees and accepts quoted_fare, not a live-updating one

This creates a real tradeoff: between quote and match (seconds, per Lesson 3's dispatch flow), the true supply/demand ratio may have shifted — Uber eats that gap rather than re-quoting mid-flow. It's a deliberate consistency-vs-freshness tradeoff: the price is "consistent" (locked) from the rider's perspective even though the underlying signal is only eventually accurate.

💡 Interview signal: Calling out that the fare is locked at quote time — and stating explicitly who absorbs the risk if conditions change before match — is the kind of product-aware detail that distinguishes a strong answer from one that only describes the pricing formula.

5. Real-Time vs. Batch: What Goes Where

ComponentReal-time or batch?Why
Per-cell multiplier (drives live fares)Real-time (stream, seconds of lag)Directly affects every fare quote; staleness is user-visible
Surge-pattern forecasting (e.g., anticipate Friday 6pm demand)Batch (nightly/hourly job)Feeds pre-positioning driver incentives, not an individual fare — tolerates hours of lag
Fairness/audit reporting (did surge disproportionately affect a region?)BatchRetrospective analysis over large time windows, not latency-sensitive

Key Takeaways

  • Surge is a windowed demand:supply ratio per geo-cell, not an instantaneous snapshot — the 5-minute window and exponential smoothing both exist to prevent noisy, flickering prices
  • The multiplier formula needs an explicit cap for business/regulatory reasons, not just a raw ratio
  • The same hot/cold split from Lesson 2 applies again: real-time stream aggregation feeds the live multiplier cache, batch jobs handle forecasting and audit over the same event stream
  • Fares are locked at quote time — a deliberate tradeoff that favors rider trust over pricing freshness during the match window

Interview Tips

  • Give the concrete formula (ratio → log-compressed, capped, smoothed multiplier) rather than saying "we compute surge based on demand"
  • Reuse the geohash cells from the location index (Lesson 2) as the pricing granularity — don't invent a separate spatial partitioning
  • State explicitly that price is locked at quote time, and name who bears the risk if conditions shift before the ride is matched
  • Separate what must be real-time (the live multiplier) from what can be batch (forecasting, audits) — putting everything in one pipeline is a common overreach
