Deep Dive — Video Encoding & Adaptive Bitrate

lesson
⏱ 14 min
📖 Chapter 22, Lesson 3

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the DAG transcoding architecture: why splitting a 90-min video into 540 ten-second segments and encoding in parallel reduces wall-clock from 6 hours to 60 seconds
  • Design the resolution ladder (7 levels × 3 codecs = 21 versions) and calculate the codec economics: AV1 50% savings × 26 GB/s egress = $2B+/year
  • Describe DASH adaptive bitrate: manifest structure, segment boundaries, keyframe forcing, client buffer management, and quality-switch logic
  • Explain why forced keyframes at segment boundaries are mandatory for DASH — and what breaks without them

1. The Transcoding Problem — Why Sequential Fails

Naive approach: one worker encodes the full video end-to-end.

90-minute video at real-time encode speed (1× CPU):      90 minutes
With GPU (10× speedup):                                   9 minutes
With multiple codecs/resolutions (21 versions):           9 × 21 = 189 minutes sequential

1,000 videos/minute uploaded → need to transcode < 1 minute per video to keep up
Sequential pipeline falls 189× behind → upload queue grows unboundedly

Root cause: treating a video as a single atomic unit when it's actually
            a time-series of independent frames.

2. DAG Transcoding — The Parallel Architecture

Key insight: a video can be split into independent segments.
             Each segment can be encoded independently and in parallel.
             Segments only depend on the split step — they're independent of each other.

DAG (Directed Acyclic Graph) for a 90-minute video:

  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │                         RAW VIDEO (S3)                               │
  └──────────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────┘
                                 │
                          ┌──────▼──────┐
                          │   SPLITTER  │  Splits into 10-second segments
                          │  (1 worker) │  540 segments for 90-min video
                          └──────┬──────┘
         ┌──────────────────────┼──────────────────────┐
         ▼                      ▼                      ▼
  ┌─────────────┐        ┌─────────────┐        ┌─────────────┐
  │ Seg_0 tasks │        │ Seg_1 tasks │  ...   │ Seg_539 tasks│
  │ ×21 versions│        │ ×21 versions│        │ ×21 versions│
  └──────┬──────┘        └──────┬──────┘        └──────┬──────┘
         │                      │                      │
         ▼                      ▼                      ▼
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │                   S3 Transcoded Segments                              │
  │    /video_id/144p/seg_000.mp4  ... /video_id/4k_av1/seg_539.mp4     │
  └──────────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────┘
                                 │
                          ┌──────▼──────┐
                          │  ASSEMBLER  │  Generates DASH .mpd + HLS .m3u8 manifests
                          │  (1 worker) │
                          └─────────────┘

Task count: 540 segments × 21 versions = 11,340 parallel tasks
Parallelism: GPU cluster with 200 GPUs × 10 tasks/GPU = 2,000 concurrent tasks
Wall clock time:
  Split:      15 seconds (sequential, but fast — just file seek + copy)
  Transcode:  all 11,340 tasks submitted simultaneously; limited by cluster throughput
              First task completes in ~15 seconds (GPU-accelerated 10s segment)
              Steady state: all tasks done in ~30–60 seconds
  Assemble:   5 seconds
  Total:      ~60 seconds for 90-minute video
  vs. sequential: 189 minutes = 189× speedup

Codec Economics — Why AV1 Matters

Codec evolution:
  H.264 (AVC):  released 2003; universal hardware decode support; reference codec
  VP9:          Google 2013; ~30–40% smaller than H.264; Chrome + Android support
  AV1:          open-source 2018 (Google/Netflix/Amazon alliance); ~50% smaller than H.264;
                hardware decode: modern Chromebooks, Android 10+, newer iPhones

AV1 encode cost:
  AV1 is 5–10× slower to encode than H.264 (even on GPU)
  → AV1 is only viable with GPU-accelerated parallel transcoding
  → Justifies the DAG architecture even more (sequential would make AV1 impractical)

AV1 savings math (YouTube scale):
  Total CDN egress:           26 GB/s (1B hours/day × 5 Mbps ÷ 8)
  % of traffic served by AV1: ~30% (modern clients; H.264 for legacy devices)
  AV1 saves vs H.264:         50%
  Net egress reduction:       26 × 0.30 × 0.50 = 3.9 GB/s
  Daily savings:              3.9 GB/s × 86,400 × $0.02/GB = $6.7M/day
  Annual savings:             $6.7M × 365 = $2.45B/year

  VP9 (30% of traffic, 35% smaller than H.264):
    Additional savings:       26 × 0.30 × 0.35 × $0.02/GB × 86,400 × 365 = $1.6B/year

  Combined codec savings:     $2.45B + $1.6B ≈ $4B/year
  (YouTube claims ~$10B/year including ISP peering + GGC; codec savings are a major component)

4. Adaptive Bitrate Streaming — DASH Deep Dive

Why Fixed Bitrate Fails

Fixed bitrate (HTTP progressive download):
  Creator uploads 1080p video → viewer downloads full file linearly
  Problem 1: slow network → constant rebuffering (no lower quality fallback)
  Problem 2: fast network → wastes bandwidth (only 1080p available regardless of need)
  Problem 3: seeking → re-download from seek point → long seek latency

DASH (Dynamic Adaptive Streaming over HTTP):
  Video pre-segmented into 10-second chunks at each quality level
  Manifest lists all quality levels + each segment's URL
  Client dynamically switches quality segment-by-segment based on bandwidth
  → No rebuffering (player can always get lower quality)
  → No wasted bandwidth (player selects only what it can consume)
  → Fast seek: jump to any segment (segments are independent; keyframe at each start)

DASH Manifest Structure

<?xml version="1.0"?>
<MPD xmlns="urn:mpeg:dash:schema:mpd:2011" mediaPresentationDuration="PT1H30M">
  <Period duration="PT1H30M">
    <AdaptationSet mimeType="video/mp4" codecs="avc1.640028">

      <!-- 1080p H.264 -->
      <Representation id="1080p" bandwidth="5000000" width="1920" height="1080">
        <SegmentTemplate
          timescale="1000"
          initialization="https://cdn.youtube.com/vid123/1080p/init.mp4"
          media="https://cdn.youtube.com/vid123/1080p/seg_$Number$.mp4"
          startNumber="0" duration="10000"/>   <!-- 10-second segments (10000ms) -->
      </Representation>

      <!-- 720p H.264 -->
      <Representation id="720p" bandwidth="2500000" width="1280" height="720">
        <SegmentTemplate
          media="https://cdn.youtube.com/vid123/720p/seg_$Number$.mp4"
          startNumber="0" duration="10000"/>
      </Representation>

      <!-- ... additional qualities ... -->

    </AdaptationSet>
  </Period>
</MPD>

Keyframe Forcing — Why It's Mandatory

Video frame types:
  I-frame (keyframe): complete, self-contained frame. Can be decoded independently.
  P-frame:            encoded as delta from previous frame. Requires preceding frames.
  B-frame:            bidirectional delta. Requires frames before AND after.

Problem without forced keyframes:
  Encoder places keyframes based on scene change detection: e.g. at seconds 8.3, 19.7, 31.2
  Segment boundaries: 0s, 10s, 20s, 30s, ...
  Seg 1 ends at 10s. Next I-frame is at 19.7s in segment 2.
  Seg 2 starts at 10s with P-frames — cannot decode segment 2 without segment 1!
  → Quality switch from 1080p to 720p at segment boundary is IMPOSSIBLE
  → Seeking to second 20 requires downloading from second 8.3 (last keyframe) → terrible seek UX

Solution: force keyframe at every segment boundary:
  ffmpeg option: -force_key_frames "expr:gte(t, n_forced*10)"
  → Keyframe inserted exactly every 10 seconds, regardless of scene content
  → Each 10-second segment is independently decodable
  → Quality switch at any segment boundary: client simply requests next segment at different quality
  → Seek: jump directly to the segment containing the target timestamp → decode from its keyframe

Cost of forced keyframes:
  Natural I-frames: ~10% of frames at typical scene change rate
  Forced I-frames every 10s at 30fps: 1 I-frame per 300 frames
  I-frames are ~10× larger than P-frames (no delta compression)
  Overhead: ~3–5% bitrate increase vs. unconstrained keyframe placement
  Trade-off: 3–5% bitrate cost for seamless quality switching and fast seeking — clearly worth it

Client ABR Algorithm

Buffer-based ABR (simplified):

state = {
    buffer_seconds: 0,      # seconds of video buffered ahead
    bandwidth_bps: 0,       # estimated network throughput (EWMA)
    current_quality: "720p"
}

def select_next_segment_quality():
    # Update bandwidth estimate (exponentially weighted moving average)
    download_time = measure_last_segment_download()
    segment_bytes = segment_size_bytes
    sample_bw = segment_bytes × 8 / download_time
    bandwidth_bps = 0.7 × bandwidth_bps + 0.3 × sample_bw  # EWMA α=0.3

    # Buffer health determines quality target
    if buffer_seconds < 5:     # buffer critical
        target = "360p"         # drop quality aggressively to prevent rebuffer
    elif buffer_seconds < 15:  # buffer normal
        target = highest quality where bitrate × 1.5 <= bandwidth_bps  # safety margin
    else:                       # buffer healthy
        target = highest quality where bitrate × 1.2 <= bandwidth_bps  # opportunistic upgrade

    return target

# Player loop:
while video_playing:
    if buffer_seconds < 30:  # keep 30s ahead
        quality = select_next_segment_quality()
        segment = fetch_segment(current_position, quality)
        append_to_buffer(segment)
        buffer_seconds += 10  # each segment = 10 seconds
    sleep(1)  # check every second

5. Two-Tower Recommendation — Watch Time Target

Why not CTR (Click-Through Rate)?
  CTR = clicks / impressions
  Optimizing CTR → maximize clicks → create misleading "clickbait" thumbnails
  Result: user clicks, watches 5 seconds, leaves → bad experience, repeat × 10 = user churns
  YouTube had this problem pre-2012: titles like "YOU WON'T BELIEVE WHAT HAPPENS AT 2:38"

Why watch time regression?
  Target: predict P(user u watches fraction f of video v)
  Score = P(f > 0.5) × duration  ← penalizes watching 100% of a 5-second video over 50% of 1-hour video
  Result: YouTube surfaces long, genuinely engaging videos → higher advertiser value + user satisfaction

Two-tower DNN architecture:
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │                        TWO-TOWER DNN                                             │
  │                                                                                  │
  │  User Tower:                          Video Tower:                               │
  │  input: user_id + features            input: video_id + features                 │
  │  - watch history embeddings           - title/description embeddings             │
  │  - demographics (age, location)       - tags, category                           │
  │  - device type                        - avg watch fraction (historical)          │
  │  - time of day                        - engagement rate (like/view ratio)        │
  │  - recent search terms                - upload recency                           │
  │          │                                    │                                  │
  │          ▼                                    ▼                                  │
  │   ┌──────────────┐                   ┌──────────────┐                           │
  │   │  DNN Layers  │                   │  DNN Layers  │                           │
  │   │  (6 layers,  │                   │  (6 layers,  │                           │
  │   │  ReLU)       │                   │  ReLU)       │                           │
  │   └──────┬───────┘                   └──────┬───────┘                           │
  │          │                                   │                                   │
  │          ▼ 256-dim user embedding            ▼ 256-dim video embedding           │
  │   ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐                      │
  │   │         Dot product (similarity score)               │                      │
  │   └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘                      │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Candidate generation (offline, hourly):
  800M video embeddings indexed in ScaNN (Scalable Nearest Neighbors)
  For user u: ScaNN.search(user_vec_u, k=500) → 500 candidate videos in ~10ms
  Stored in Redis: candidates:{user_id} with TTL=1 hour

Real-time ranking (per feed request, < 100ms):
  Fetch 500 candidates from Redis
  Score each with lightweight neural ranker (smaller model, fast inference)
  Features: fresh signals (watch history last 1h, current search context, trending)
  Output: ranked list → top 10 returned to client
  Diversity enforcement: max 2 videos per channel in top 10

Key Takeaways

  • DAG parallelism = 189× speedup: 11,340 tasks for 90-min video submitted simultaneously; wall clock ≈ 1 task time (60s) vs sequential 189 minutes
  • AV1 savings = $2.45B/year: 50% smaller × 30% of traffic × 26 GB/s egress — the codec choice is an economic decision at this scale
  • Forced keyframes are mandatory: without them, segment boundaries don't have I-frames → quality switch is impossible → adaptive bitrate breaks entirely
  • Buffer-based ABR: target = keep 30s ahead; quality = highest bitrate where bitrate × 1.5 safety factor ≤ estimated bandwidth; aggressive downgrade when buffer < 5s
  • Watch time beats CTR: score = P(watch fraction > 50%) × duration; this prevents both clickbait (CTR) and rabbit-hole optimization (raw watch time without satisfaction signal)

Interview Tips

  • The DAG transcoding insight is THE answer to "how do you process 500 hrs/min?" — say "split into segments, encode all in parallel, wall-clock ≈ 1 segment time regardless of video length"
  • When asked about adaptive bitrate: "the key enabler is forced keyframes at every segment boundary — without them, segments are not independently decodable and quality switching breaks"
  • AV1 is a great follow-up point: "AV1 is 5–10× slower to encode than H.264, which is exactly why we need the DAG parallel architecture — sequential encoding would make AV1 impractical at 500 hrs/min"
  • For recommendation: proactively explain the CTR problem — "optimizing CTR alone creates a click-bait incentive; YouTube shifted to watch-time regression in 2012, which aligns creator incentives with viewer satisfaction"
  • Mention the satisfaction signal: "beyond watch time, YouTube surveys a sample of users post-video ('Did you enjoy this?') to catch 'rabbit hole' content that maximizes watch time but leaves users feeling bad"
